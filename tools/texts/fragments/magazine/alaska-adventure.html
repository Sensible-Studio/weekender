The plan fitted on the back of a boarding pass: two weeks of skiing from a camp on a glacier, then a few days with fly rods on a river near the coast before the flight home. Four of us had talked about Alaska for years, always for one thing or the other. It was our pilot, on the phone in January, who asked why we wanted to choose. The snow is at its best in April, he said, and that is when the first steelhead come into the rivers. Bring both bags.

He put us down on the ice on a morning with no wind, unloaded sleds, fuel and three weeks of food in ten minutes, and left. The sound of the engine took a long time to fade, and then there was none at all. Camp was two sleeping tents and a cook tent dug a metre into the snow, with a wall of cut blocks on the weather side. The nearest road was a day's flying away in bad conditions and forty minutes in good ones. For eighteen days that was our address.

Skiing there is slow work. Every line starts with a walk on skins, roped together where the glacier is broken, with a probe in the pack and one eye on the slope above. We climbed in the cold of the morning and skied when the sun had softened the top few centimetres, and on most days we managed one long run, sometimes two. I counted nine days of skiing and nine days in the tent waiting for the cloud to lift. Nobody complained. You read, you cook, you dig out the tents again, and you learn every card game the others know.

The run I will remember came on day fourteen. A ramp of snow on the north side of the valley had been in shadow all trip, and we had looked at it every evening from the cook tent. That morning it was clear and still. Three hours up, the last hundred metres on crampons with skis on our backs, and then a descent of almost a thousand metres in snow so light that it closed behind us. At the bottom we sat on our packs and nobody said anything for a while.

The plane came back on the first good morning after that and flew us out to a fishing village on the gulf coast, where the air smelled of spruce and low tide and felt almost warm. We traded ski boots for waders in a car park. The river was the colour of tea and no wider than a road, and the fish in it were fresh from the sea, bright silver and far stronger than they looked. I landed two in three days and lost several more. One of us, who had never held a fly rod before the trip, caught the biggest fish of the week on his second morning and has not stopped mentioning it.

People ask which half was better, and I do not have an answer. The glacier was the hardest and quietest place I have been, and the river was pure pleasure after it: green banks, eagles in the trees, a diner that opened at five for the fishermen. What I would say is that two weeks is the right length. It takes that long to stop counting days, and on the ice that is when the trip really begins.
