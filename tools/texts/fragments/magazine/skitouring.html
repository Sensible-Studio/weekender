For twenty winters I thought I knew what skiing was. You queue, you sit on a chair, you ski down, you queue again, and at the end of the day you count the runs. Then a friend lent me a pair of touring bindings and a set of skins, strips of fabric that stick to the base of the ski and grip the snow, and took me up a valley with no lifts in it at all. We climbed for three hours and skied for twelve minutes. I have not counted runs since.

The first thing you notice is the quiet. Away from the machinery a winter mountain makes almost no sound, only the slide of your own skis and your breathing, which is louder than you would like. The second is the pace. Touring is walking, steady and unhurried, at a speed that lets you talk. You see the tracks of a hare, the line where the wind has stripped a ridge, the way the snow changes from one side of a gully to the other. On a chairlift all of that goes past underneath you.

It also asks more of you. Nobody has checked the slope or marked the rocks, so the judgment is yours. Everyone in the group carries a transceiver, a shovel and a probe and knows how to use them, and before my second season I spent three days on an avalanche course that changed how I look at every hill. We read the bulletin each morning, we turn back when something feels wrong, and we have turned back often. A summit that we did not reach has never spoiled a day.

And then there is the descent. You take off the skins at the top with cold fingers, drink something hot, lock your heels down and look at a slope with no tracks on it but the ones you made coming up. It may be perfect powder or it may be a crust that fights every turn. Either way you earned it, and it tastes different. If you ski well on a piste and are curious, hire the gear, book a guide for a day and go and see. Start small. Start soon.
