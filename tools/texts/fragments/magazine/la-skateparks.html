Modern skateboarding grew up in and around Los Angeles. In the dry summers of the 1970s kids from the beach towns climbed fences to ride empty swimming pools, and the moves they worked out there are still the grammar of the sport. Half a century later the city has more public concrete to skate than anyone could cover in a month. I spent two weeks trying anyway, with a board, a bus pass and a list that kept getting longer. These are the places I would send a friend.

Venice Beach. Start here, because everyone does. The park sits on the sand beside the boardwalk, with the ocean behind it and a crowd along the rail from morning to dusk. It opened in 2009 and has a snake run, a street section and two bowls, the deeper of which is not a place to learn. Go before nine if you want room to move. Go at sunset if you want to watch people who have skated it every day for years.

The plazas of the Westside. A short ride inland the city has built several street plazas: ledges, stairs, rails and banks laid out like a tidy version of the downtown spots that skaters used to be chased from. Stoner Plaza in West Los Angeles is the best known. They are free, they are lit in the evening, and the level is high but friendly. Wait your turn, say hello, and nobody will mind that you are new.

Santa Monica. The Cove is a city-run park with a deep pool, a long flowing bowl and a street course, and it is one of the few places on this list with staff, a small entry fee and rules about helmets. That makes it calmer than the open parks, and a good choice if you are skating with children or coming back to a board after a long break.

South to Huntington Beach. It is outside the city, about an hour down the coast, and worth the trip. The park built there by a well-known shoe company has a large street plaza and a combi bowl copied from a famous one of the 1980s. Entry is free and protective gear is required in the bowl. Take the coast road back and stop wherever you see boards leaning against a wall.

East of the river. The parks of East Los Angeles and the San Gabriel Valley get fewer visitors and deserve more. They tend to be smaller, older and full of local kids who are frighteningly good. Belvedere, with its bowls and pump bumps beside a lake, was my favourite afternoon of the trip, helped by the taco truck parked at the gate.

A few things I learned the hard way. Concrete gets hot by noon from June to September, so skate early and late. Carry water and a skate tool. Most parks ask for a helmet and some enforce it. And look up the park the night before you go: resurfacing, contests and film shoots close them more often than you would expect in a city this devoted to skating.
