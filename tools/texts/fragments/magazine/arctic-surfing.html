The car thermometer says minus four when we park above the bay, and nobody moves to open a door. Through the windscreen the swell is doing exactly what the forecast promised: clean lines, head high, wrapping around the point and peeling across a beach with no footprints on it. It is half past ten in the morning in February, the sun has been up for forty minutes, and it will be gone again by early afternoon. In the north of Norway, in winter, you surf when there is light. So we open the doors.

Getting dressed is the hard part of the day. A six millimetre suit with a hood, boots, mittens with one finger free, and a flask of warm water poured down the neck before the walk to the shore. People imagine that the sea here is unbearable. It is around five degrees, which the suit handles well; what hurts is the wind on a wet face, and the first duck dive, when the cold finds the only skin you left uncovered. After that there is a strange comfort in it. The water is clear enough to see the stones on the bottom, the mountains go straight up from the beach, and snow comes down to the high tide line.

We shared the bay with four people all week. Two were brothers from the village who learned on boards that their father brought home from a fishing season abroad, and who now run a small shed renting suits to visitors like us. They do not talk about their wave as a secret. They talk about it the way farmers talk about a field: when it works, what the wind does to it, which rock to line up with. On the second day one of them paddled over and moved me twenty metres up the point without a word. The next set came straight to me.

The days fall into a rhythm that has little to do with a clock. Coffee in the dark, a look at the bay with a torch, two hours in the water around noon, then soup, a sauna if someone has lit one, and wet gear hung over every radiator in the cabin. By four it is night. We wax boards, read the charts and go outside every half hour to look up, because on clear evenings the sky does what we came this far north hoping to see. The first green arc showed on our third night, low over the ridge, and within ten minutes it had spread across the whole fjord.

I have surfed warmer and better waves, and I have never been as content after a session as I was on that beach, shaking, trying to undo a zip with fingers that would not close. Cold water takes away the crowd, the noise and most of the vanity that goes with surfing. What is left is simple: a few friends, a wave that asks for effort, and light that you learn to treat as something precious. We are going back next winter, a week later in the season, for one more hour of sun.
