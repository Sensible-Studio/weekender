From the window of a small plane the forest has no edges. For two hours there is only green, cut now and then by a brown river that doubles back on itself, and then the pilot points at a strip of red earth the length of a football pitch and starts to descend. The whole village had come out to watch us land. That is how the trip began, and it set the tone for the ten days that followed: everything here happens in public, and nothing happens fast.

We had come for peacock bass, a fish that fly anglers talk about with a mix of affection and alarm. It is not a bass at all but a cichlid, gold and green with three black bars and a spot on the tail like an eye, and it hits a fly as if it holds a grudge. The river we fished is a blackwater tributary far from the main stream, reached by a day in a long wooden canoe with an outboard, and it is open to a handful of visitors each dry season by agreement with the community that lives on it.

Camp moved every two days. Hammocks under a tarp, a fire, a table made on the spot from saplings and vine. The guides were men from the village who had fished this water since they could walk, and they handled the canoes with a precision that I never got used to. A guide would cut the engine, stand on the bow with a pole, and slide us without a sound into a lagoon that I had not even seen from the river.

The fishing itself is hot, physical and short-range. You stand in the bow and throw a large, bright streamer on a heavy line as close to the sunken timber as you dare, then strip it back as fast as you can. Most casts bring nothing. Then the water bulges behind the fly, and what follows is closer to a collision than a bite. My first good fish ran straight into a fallen tree and broke me off in three seconds. The guide laughed, handed me a thicker leader and said one word, which I later learned means again.

By the fourth day I had learned to keep the rod low and pull hard, and the fish started coming to the boat. We released every one of them. The largest was a little over six kilos, held for a moment in both hands beside the canoe, its colours brighter than seemed reasonable, before it kicked and was gone into water the colour of strong tea.

Not everything in the river is a peacock bass. We caught piranha, which the cooks were happy to see, a toothed silver fish called a payara that looks like something drawn by a child after a nightmare, and once a small catfish with whiskers longer than its body. Pink river dolphins followed the canoe on two mornings. At night caiman eyes shone red along the bank in the light of a head torch, and howler monkeys woke us before dawn with a sound like wind in a tunnel.

What stays with me most is the afternoon we spent in the village on the way out. We were shown the school, a manioc garden and the house where flour is toasted over a fire on a pan two metres wide. The children wanted to see the flies, and went through the box with great seriousness, naming the fish that each one would catch. An old man told us, through the guide, that the river had been poorer when he was young and outsiders came to net it. The visitors with their odd rods, he said, are easier neighbours.

I do not want to make it sound simple. The heat is heavy, the insects are tireless, and a small cut needs care for a week. You are a long way from a doctor. We drank a great deal of water, wore long sleeves from dusk and did what the guides said, and we were fine. Anyone who goes should do the same and should go with an outfit that works with the community, not around it.

On the last morning the plane was three hours late, and nobody minded. We sat in the shade by the airstrip with the men who had guided us, passing round the photographs on a phone, and I understood that I had spent ten days without once knowing the time. The fish were the reason to go. The river, and the people who let us onto it, are the reason I would go back.
