We picked up the van in Perth on a Monday: a high-roofed thing with a bed, a two-ring stove, a fridge the size of a shoebox and three hundred thousand kilometres on the clock. The man at the depot showed us how to change a wheel and gave us one piece of advice, which was never to drive at dusk, because that is when the kangaroos come to the road. Then we turned north with three weeks ahead of us and no booking anywhere. The road from there runs for four thousand kilometres up the coast, and in all that distance there are only a few towns large enough to have a traffic light.

Distance is the first thing to understand about the west. The map shows a town and you imagine a town; what you find is a fuel pump, a shop and a pub, and the next one is two hours on. Between them the land is red and flat and covered in low grey scrub, and the road is so straight that an oncoming truck is visible for minutes before it passes. We learned to fill the tank and the water at every chance, to wave at every driver, and to love the roadhouses, where a toasted sandwich at eleven in the morning can feel like an event.

And then, again and again, the desert stops at a beach. We snorkelled over coral a few steps from the sand at a reef that runs for hundreds of kilometres along the shore, and on one morning swam, at a respectful distance, beside a whale shark as long as our van. Further south, dolphins come into knee-deep water at dawn. Inland there is a national park of gorges cut into rock the colour of rust, where we walked down ladders of stone to green pools and had them to ourselves. I surfed twice, badly, at a point break near a town that seemed to exist only for that wave.

Evenings were the best of it. You find a place to stop, level the van with a couple of stones, light the stove and watch the sky go from orange to a blue that is nearly black. With no town for a hundred kilometres the stars come right down to the horizon, and the Milky Way is bright enough to cast a faint shadow. Most nights we shared a camp with one or two other vans. People in the west talk to strangers easily, and we were given fish, directions, a spare fuse and more than one cold drink by people whose names we never learned.

Would I change anything? I would take a second spare wheel, since we needed the first. I would go in the southern autumn or spring, when the north is neither too hot nor cut off by rain. And I would take longer. Three weeks sounded generous in Perth, and by the end we were skipping places that deserved days. The van went back with another five thousand kilometres on it, red dust in every seam, and two people in the front seats who had already started planning the east coast.
